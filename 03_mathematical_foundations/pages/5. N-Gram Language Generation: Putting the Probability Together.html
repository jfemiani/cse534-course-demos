Build your first complete language generator

An N-gram model predicts what comes next by looking at the previous few items. The letter N tells you how many items are considered together. In a character bigram model, N is 2: the model uses one character as the context and predicts the next character.

For example, if the character b is followed by a three times and by y once in the training text, the model estimates a 3/4 chance of a and a 1/4 chance of y after b. Generating text means repeatedly sampling from these learned probabilities.

This demonstration connects earlier lessons: counts become conditional probabilities, sampling chooses the next character, and negative log-likelihood measures how well the model predicts a sequence.

Chaining conditional probabilities

To generate a complete sequence, we chain conditional probabilities using the product rule. The probability of a sequence x1, x2, ..., xT factors as:

p(x1,x2,…,xT)=p(x1)∏t=2Tp(xt|x1,…,xt−1)

The large Π symbol (capital pi, “PIE”) means multiply. Each term p(xt | x1, ..., xt−1) is the probability of the next character given all previous characters. This is exact—no approximation yet.

The Markov assumption: trading accuracy for tractability

The full conditional p(xt | x1, ..., xt−1) depends on the entire history. With limited training data, most long contexts appear rarely or never, making accurate estimation impossible. The Markov assumption restricts each prediction to a fixed context window of length N−1:

p(xt|x1,…,xt−1)≈p(xt|xt−N+1,…,xt−1)

A bigram model (N = 2) sees only one preceding character. Larger N can model longer dependencies but requires exponentially more training data and produces sparser probability estimates. This tradeoff between model capacity and data requirements is fundamental to statistical modeling.

Inductive bias: what the model cannot learn

The fixed context window imposes a structural constraint on what patterns the model can represent. This is called an inductive bias—the model's architecture fundamentally limits what it can learn, regardless of how much training data is available.

The model cannot learn:

  • Long-range dependencies: Matching opening and closing quotes separated by hundreds of characters
  • Palindromes: No understanding of symmetry or reversibility
  • Hierarchical structure: Nested parentheses or balanced brackets
  • Global coherence: Maintaining a consistent topic across an entire paragraph
  • Pattern repetition limits: Training on "bonbon" can produce "bonbonbonbon..." indefinitely with no stopping mechanism
  • Sparse contexts: With an 8-character context, rare phrases like "To be or" may appear too infrequently to learn reliable continuations
  • Dead ends: Generating a typo or rare prefix with no continuation in training data causes the model to stop

Modern neural language models (transformers) relax these constraints through learned attention mechanisms that can attend to distant context. However, even transformers have finite context windows, though much larger than traditional N-grams.

From counts to conditional probabilities

For a context c and possible next character x, Equation (5.1) estimates the conditional probability from counts. Read p̂ as “p hat”; the hat says this probability is estimated from the training text. The numerator counts how often x followed c. The denominator counts every occasion on which c had a following character. Dividing makes the estimated probabilities for all possible next characters add to 1.

p^(x|c)=count(c,x)count(c)
(5.1)

A bigram character model uses one preceding character as context. A larger N-gram uses a longer context. Longer contexts can capture more structure, but many contexts may occur rarely or never. One implementation detail matters when you run the course code: ORDER is the context length, not the conventional value of N. Therefore, ORDER = 1 produces a bigram model (N = 2), ORDER = 2 produces a trigram model (N = 3), and so on.

Generation

  1. Begin with an initial context.
  2. Look up the counts of characters that followed that context in the training text.
  3. Normalize the counts into probabilities.
  4. Sample the next character.
  5. Append it, update the context, and repeat.

This is a genuine generative model. It is small and count-based rather than neural, but it defines and samples from a conditional probability distribution.

Evaluation and Bayesian smoothing

The negative log-likelihood of held-out text adds the surprise assigned to each actual next character. In Equation (5.2), T is the number of characters being predicted, t identifies one position, ct is the context at that position, and xt is the character that actually followed it. The ∑ symbol—sigma ("SIG-ma")—adds the losses, and dividing by T gives the average. exp means the exponential function; it reverses the natural logarithm used in the loss. Lower negative log-likelihood and lower perplexity both mean that the model assigned greater probability to the held-out sequence.

NLLavg=−1T∑t=1Tlogp(xt|ct)perplexity=exp(NLLavg)
(5.2)

Bayesian smoothing: prior and posterior

An unseen context–character pair would otherwise receive probability zero. Its logarithm would then be undefined, producing an infinite loss. To handle this, we use Bayesian smoothing with a small uniform prior. The smoothed probability in Equation (5.3) adds a small pseudo-count α (e.g., 0.01) to both numerator and denominator:

p^(x|c)=count(c,x)+αcount(c)+α⋅|vocab|
(5.3)

From a Bayesian perspective:

  • Prior: A uniform distribution assigns α pseudo-counts to every character (our belief before seeing data)
  • Likelihood: The observed counts from training data (what we actually saw)
  • Posterior: The smoothed probability combines prior and likelihood (updated belief after seeing data)

This prevents evaluation from collapsing due to unseen pairs. Setting α = 0.01 rather than α = 1 (add-one or Laplace smoothing) trusts the observed data more and assigns less weight to unseen events, preventing overly uniform predictions that would generate gibberish. The choice of α represents a tradeoff: smaller values trust the data more but handle unseen contexts less gracefully; larger values are more robust to sparsity but may oversmooth and dilute learned patterns.
