PCA and eigenfaces: compressing high-dimensional data

Lesson 7 showed that a covariance matrix describes the directions and scales of an elliptical cloud of data. This lesson applies that geometry to real image data. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the directions with the greatest variation and keeps only the most informative ones, enabling dramatic compression.

Face images contain thousands of pixels, but PCA reveals that most variation can be captured by a much smaller number of principal directions. When applied to faces, these directions are called eigenfaces.

What you will do: Load a real face dataset, compute eigenfaces via PCA, and reconstruct faces from compressed representations.

Why PCA matters for generative AI

Modern generative models like diffusion models and VAEs operate in high-dimensional pixel space, but much of that space contains nonsensical images. PCA finds the subspace where real data actually lives. Understanding this dimensionality reduction helps you grasp how generative models learn compact representations.

Image generation quality is often evaluated by comparing feature distributions in reduced-dimensional spaces (like FiD, covered in a later lesson after you understand how to compare multivariate Gaussians).

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA finds the directions of greatest variation in high-dimensional data. The algorithm:

  1. Center the data by subtracting mean vector μ
  2. Compute the covariance matrix
  3. Find its eigenvectors (directions that transform without rotation)
  4. Sort by eigenvalue (variance along that direction), largest first
  5. Keep the first k directions to compress data
z=QkT(x−μ)
(8.1)

In Equation (8.1), columns of Qk are the first k principal directions. The projection gives reduced coordinates z. Each eigenvalue tells how much variance that direction captures. Keeping the first k directions preserves as much variance as possible among all k-dimensional linear projections.

Eigenfaces: PCA on face images

A grayscale face image with h × w pixels can be treated as a vector with h · w dimensions. For 50x37 images, that is 1,850 dimensions. PCA finds recurring patterns across many face images.

The eigenvectors, when reshaped back to images, are called eigenfaces. Each eigenface captures a mode of variation (e.g., lighting direction, facial hair, glasses). A face can be represented as:

face≈mean face+∑i=1kzi⋅eigenfacei

Instead of storing 1,850 numbers, we store k ≈ 150 coefficients plus the shared eigenfaces. This is lossy compression: reconstructions are approximate but preserve recognizable features.
