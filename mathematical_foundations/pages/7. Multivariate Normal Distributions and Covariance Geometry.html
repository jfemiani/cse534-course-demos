A one-dimensional normal distribution describes one number. A multivariate normal distribution describes several related numbers at once. For example, one generative-AI request might be described by both its output length and its response time. Those numbers are stored together in a vector, which is simply an ordered list of values.

This lesson leans on vectors, matrices, and a few linear-algebra ideas—transpose, determinant, inverse, eigenvectors. If those terms feel rusty, that is expected and fine. Each one is explained in plain language the first time it appears, and the Linear algebra refresher section at the bottom links to excellent free lectures and a textbook if you want a deeper review.

A covariance matrix records both the spread of each measurement and how pairs of measurements change together. Its diagonal entries are variances. An off-diagonal entry is a covariance: a positive value means the two measurements tend to rise together, a negative value means one tends to fall as the other rises, and a value near zero means there is little linear relationship. In two dimensions, this structure can be drawn as an ellipse whose width, height, and direction summarize the matrix.

Equation (7.1) defines covariance. The bold μ is the mean vector; read μ as mu (“mew”). The bold Σ is capital sigma (“SIG-ma”). Here Σ names a matrix—it is different from the ∑ summation operator. The symbol 𝔼 means expected value, which you can read here as the long-run average over many observations. Superscript T means transpose: it turns a column into a row so the multiplication produces a matrix.

Σ=𝔼[(X−μ)(X−μ)T]
(7.1)

Elliptical geometry

Different diagonal variances stretch the ellipse by different amounts along the coordinate axes. Nonzero covariance changes its principal directions, which usually appears as a rotated ellipse. Equation (7.2) is the full density. You do not need to memorize it. Read its pieces instead: d is the number of dimensions; lowercase π is pi (“pie”); |Σ| is the determinant, a single number describing overall spread; Σ−1 is the inverse covariance, which adjusts distance for the ellipse’s scale and direction; and exp makes points farther from μ receive lower density.

p(x;μ,Σ)=1(2π)d2|Σ|12exp(−12(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ))
(7.2)

Transform standard-normal noise

Equation (7.3) gives a practical recipe for generating correlated values. Start with a standard-normal vector z. The bold I is the identity matrix, meaning the starting coordinates have variance 1 and no covariance. Multiplying by matrix A scales and mixes the coordinates; adding μ translates the center. The condition AAT = Σ ensures that the generated vector has the desired covariance.

z∼𝒩(0,I)x=μ+Az,AAT=Σ
(7.3)

Equation (7.4) shows a two-dimensional rotation matrix. The symbol θ is theta (pronounced “THAY-ta”) and represents the rotation angle. sin and cos are the sine and cosine functions. A rotation changes orientation without changing lengths. Rotating backward undoes the rotation, so the inverse matrix R−1 is the same as the transpose RT.

R(θ)=(cosθ−sinθsinθcosθ)R−1=RT
(7.4)

An eigenvector identifies a direction that the covariance transformation stretches without turning away from that direction. Its eigenvalue tells how much variance lies along that direction, so the square root of the eigenvalue is the standard deviation there. The full derivation is optional. The required idea is that standard-normal noise can be scaled, rotated, and translated into a Gaussian with a chosen center, spread, and orientation.
