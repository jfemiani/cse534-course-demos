A one-dimensional normal distribution describes one number. A multivariate normal distribution describes several related numbers at once. Foster’s Generative Deep Learning opens with exactly this idea: a biscuit tin can be described by both its height and its diameter. Those two numbers are stored together in a vector, which is simply an ordered list of values.

This lesson leans on vectors, matrices, and a few linear-algebra ideas—transpose, determinant, inverse, eigenvectors. If those terms feel rusty, that is expected and fine. Each one is explained in plain language the first time it appears, and the Linear algebra refresher section at the bottom links to excellent free lectures and a textbook if you want a deeper review.

A covariance matrix records both the spread of each measurement and how pairs of measurements change together.

Two independent measurements

Suppose a biscuit tin is described by two numbers: its height X1 and its diameter X2. If these were unrelated—knowing one told you nothing about the other—each would just be its own one-dimensional normal distribution, exactly like the ones from Lesson 6: X1 ~ 𝒩(μ1, σ12) and X2 ~ 𝒩(μ2, σ22).

To find the probability of seeing a particular (height, diameter) pair, multiply the two one-dimensional densities together—equation (7.1). This is just the ordinary product rule for independent probabilities: how likely is this height, times how likely is this diameter.

p(x1,x2)=12πσ12exp(−(x1−μ1)22σ12)⋅12πσ22exp(−(x2−μ2)22σ22)
(7.1)

To keep the next few steps uncluttered, let v1 = X1 − μ1 and v2 = X2 − μ2—each vi is just how far that coordinate sits from its mean. Because multiplying two exponentials adds their exponents—exp(a)⋅exp(b) = exp(a + b)—the product in (7.1) collapses into a single exponential of a sum, and the two separate normalizing constants multiply into one—equation (7.2).

=12πσ1σ2exp(−12[v12σ12+v22σ22])
(7.2)

The bracketed sum is the important piece. It is the only part of the density that depends on x—the constant out front does not. When you later fit μ and Σ to data by maximum likelihood, this bracketed sum is exactly the term you differentiate: the mean and variance that maximize likelihood are the ones that make this weighted, squared distance from the data to μ as small as possible.

This bracketed sum is also exactly the quadratic form (x − μ)TΣ−1(x − μ) used in the general multivariate formula below, once Σ−1 is written as the diagonal matrix with 1/σ12 and 1/σ22 on its diagonal. Equation (7.3) carries out that matrix multiplication step by step, using v = x − μ = (v1, v2), to confirm it lands on the same bracketed sum as (7.2).

vTΣ−1v=(v1v2)(σ1−200σ2−2)(v1v2)=(v1v2)(σ1−2v1σ2−2v2)=v12σ12+v22σ22
(7.3)

Inverting a diagonal matrix just means inverting each diagonal entry, so Σ itself is diagonal with σ12 and σ22 on the diagonal—equation (7.4), the covariance matrix for two independent measurements.

Σ=(σ1200σ22)
(7.4)

Figure 7.1 shows exactly this: the joint scatter of (X1, X2) samples, flanked by the two 1D marginal density curves from equation (7.1). The scatter panel uses equal axis scaling, so its shape can be trusted—X1 has twice the standard deviation of X2, and the cloud is visibly twice as wide as it is tall, with no tilt at all, because the two measurements are independent.

Correlated dimensions

Now suppose the two measurements are related—taller biscuit tins in a manufacturer’s product line also tend to be wider. A single variance for each dimension is no longer enough; the covariance matrix needs an off-diagonal term that captures the relationship. Equation (7.5) is the general definition of covariance, for any number of dimensions and any relationship between them. An off-diagonal entry is a covariance: a positive value means the two measurements tend to rise together, a negative value means one tends to fall as the other rises, and a value near zero means little linear relationship.

Σ=𝔼[(X−μ)(X−μ)T]
(7.5)

When Σ happens to be diagonal, equation (7.5) reduces exactly to equation (7.4).

In practice Σ is estimated from data, and it helps to see what that computation actually is. Collect n samples and mean-center each one, v(i) = x(i) − μ = (v1(i), v2(i))—the same v notation from equations (7.1)–(7.3), now with one superscript (i) per sample. Stack these n centered points as the rows of an n×2 matrix V = (v(1); v(2); …; v(n))—semicolons separating rows, the same layout numpy, pandas, and scikit-learn use for a data matrix (each row one sample, each column one feature). Equation (7.6) shows that the matrix product VTV, averaged over the n samples, is exactly Σ: a 2×2 matrix whose four entries are each an average of a product of two coordinates across all n points.

Σ=1nVTV=1n∑i=1n(v12v1v2v1v2v22)
(7.6)

Each entry of the sum uses the coordinates v1 and v2 of one sample at a time: the diagonal entries average the squared deviation of each coordinate (matching the variances on the diagonal of Σ), and the off-diagonal entry averages the product of the two coordinates together (the covariance). This is the computation numpy.cov and torch.cov perform—but watch the orientation: both expect each row to be a coordinate and each column an observation, the opposite of the row-per-sample V used above, so a row-per-sample array needs a transpose first, e.g. numpy.cov(V.T).

Equation (7.7) is the density for this general case—it works whether Σ is diagonal or not. You do not need to memorize it.

p(x;μ,Σ)=1(2π)d2|Σ|12exp(−12(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ))
(7.7)

Figure 7.2 shows what changes: a nonzero covariance tilts the ellipse (right) instead of leaving it aligned with the axes (left, the same diagonal case as Figure 7.1).

Mahalanobis distance

Ordinary (Euclidean) distance from μ treats every direction equally, which is misleading once the ellipse is tilted or stretched: a point a fixed Euclidean distance away along the long axis is far more “typical” than the same distance along the short axis. The Mahalanobis distance, equation (7.8), fixes this by measuring distance in units of standard deviation along the principal axes.

DM(x)=(x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ)
(7.8)

Equivalently: rotate to the principal axes, rescale each one to have variance 1, and then measure ordinary distance. That rescaled, rotated space—where every direction has the same, unit variance—is called isotropic or standardized space. It is exactly the space standard-normal noise z ~ 𝒩(0, I) lives in. Mahalanobis distance in the original, correlated coordinates is just ordinary Euclidean distance once you have moved into that standardized space.

The name comes from the Indian statistician Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis (1893–1972), who introduced this distance in 1930 while studying anthropometric measurements, and who later founded the Indian Statistical Institute.

A method to sample multivariate normals

The isotropic space from the Mahalanobis distance discussion works in reverse too: it gives a practical recipe for generating correlated samples from any chosen μ and Σ. Start with a standard-normal vector z ~ 𝒩(0, I)—the bold I is the identity matrix, meaning the starting coordinates have variance 1 and no covariance. Suppose you could find a matrix A with the property AAT = Σ—multiplying A by its own transpose reproduces the covariance you want. Then transforming z with that A, and shifting by μ, is exactly equation (7.9)—the same reparameterization used to turn a 1D standard normal into any Normal(μ, σ²) by computing σz + μ, with A now taking the place of σ. Multiplying by A scales and mixes the coordinates, and adding μ translates the center. The condition AAT = Σ is what guarantees the generated vector ends up with the desired covariance—so the only remaining question is how to find such an A. (AAT = Σ and ATA = Σ are both valid ways to state the requirement—AAT is the one used here because it is the form that factors cleanly once A is split into a rotation and a scale, below.)

z∼𝒩(0,I)x=μ+Az,AAT=Σ
(7.9)

The natural choice for A is built from two simple pieces. First, a diagonal scale matrix S, equation (7.10), with the desired standard deviations σ1 and σ2 directly on the diagonal—not squared, since S scales standard deviations while Σ itself holds variances.

S=(σ100σ2)
(7.10)

Second, a rotation R(θ), equation (7.11), that turns the x- and y-axes to point along the directions you want the ellipse to stretch—these directions are called the principal component axes, and the next section shows they are exactly the eigenvectors of Σ.

R(θ)=(cosθ−sinθsinθcosθ)R−1=RT
(7.11)

Setting A = RS combines the two—but transposing a product reverses the order of its factors, (RS)T = STRT, not RTST. One way to see why: transpose behaves like an inverse—exactly so, for orthogonal matrices—and undoing a sequence of steps means undoing them in reverse order. If getting from room A to room C means walking through room B first, retracing your steps means leaving room B, then room A, not the other way around. The shapes agree, too: if R is m×n and S is n×p, then RS is m×p and its transpose is p×m. But RT is n×m and ST is p×n, so RTST only multiplies when m = p, while STRT—p×n times n×m—always lines up.

AAT=(RS)(RS)T=RSSTRT=RS2RT

It turns out every covariance matrix Σ can be written this way, for some rotation R aligned with its principal axes and some diagonal S holding the standard deviations along them—this is a special case of the eigendecomposition of a symmetric matrix, closely related to the singular value decomposition (SVD); see the linear algebra refresher below for resources that cover it.

Figure 7.3 shows this recipe applied step by step: standard-normal samples are stretched by the scale matrix S, rotated by R, and shifted by μ.

Notice that the order matters: scale first, in the axis-aligned frame where scaling means something (stretching along a known direction), and only then rotate into the tilted orientation you actually want. Scaling after rotating would stretch the wrong directions. A later figure will apply this same recipe to a recognizable shape rather than an anonymous scatter, so you can check that specific points end up in the correct place, not just that the final cloud looks appropriately elliptical.

Principal component axes

The rotation R and scale S used above have a name for what they capture: the directions R points the axes toward are the principal component axes (or principal axes) of Σ—the directions along which the data spreads out the most and the least, always perpendicular to each other. These are the black lines drawn on the tilted ellipse in Figure 7.2. Formally, these are the eigenvectors of Σ: a direction the covariance stretches without turning away from it. The corresponding eigenvalues give the variance along each direction. Call them σ12 and σ22—these are exactly the diagonal entries of SST from above, since S is diagonal with σ1 and σ2 on it:

SST=(σ1200σ22)

so the diagonal entries of S itself—σ1 and σ2—are the standard deviations along the principal axes: the square roots of the eigenvalues. These are also the singular values of A = RS: because R is a rotation (orthogonal, so it stretches nothing), it doesn’t change how much A stretches any direction, so A’s singular values are exactly S’s diagonal entries.

If you rotated your coordinate system to line up with the principal axes, the tilted ellipse in Figure 7.2 would turn back into an axis-aligned ellipse, exactly like Figure 7.1—just with different variances along each axis than before. That rotation is the bridge between the simple diagonal case and the general correlated case: every covariance matrix is really just a diagonal one (independent measurements), viewed from a rotated angle.

In two dimensions this rotation is exactly R(θ) from equation (7.11)—reversing it, R(−θ), undoes the turn, and undoing a rotation is the same as transposing its matrix: R(−θ) = RT = R−1. In higher dimensions, matrices with the same defining property—columns that are unit length and mutually perpendicular, which means multiplying the matrix by its own transpose gives back the identity matrix, QQT = I—are called orthogonal matrices. Orthogonal matrices are the general version of rotations, but they also include reflections: transformations that flip orientation, the way a mirror does. This distinction rarely matters for a Gaussian—reflecting through any principal axis leaves the ellipse (and the density) unchanged.

Fitting a Gaussian is PCA

Given real data, fitting a multivariate Gaussian means estimating μ and Σ—and both are things this lesson has already shown how to compute. The mean is just the sample mean, exactly as in the 1D case, only now each xi is a vector instead of a number:

μ^=1n∑i=1nxi
(7.12)

Once the mean is known, the covariance is exactly equation (7.6) from earlier—the mean-centered data matrix product (1/n)VTV. What you often want, though, is not Σ itself but its principal axes and the variances along them—that is, R and S (or, in the general orthogonal-matrix notation, Q and D). These are just the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of Σ, so finding them is a one-line call to a library function—but read the output carefully: the eigenvalues come back as variances, σ12 and σ22 (the diagonal of SST, not of S itself), so take their square root to get the standard deviations on the diagonal of S. The eigenvectors come back as the columns of a matrix, in the same order as the eigenvalues—that matrix of columns is R (or Q), matching the convention used for R(θ) in equation (7.11), whose columns are the rotated axis directions.

eigenvalues, eigenvectors = numpy.linalg.eigh(Sigma)  # Sigma is symmetric
std_devs = numpy.sqrt(eigenvalues)                    # diagonal entries of S
R = eigenvectors                                      # columns are the principal axes

This decomposition—splitting a covariance matrix into a rotation (the principal axes) and a scale (the variance along each axis)—is exactly the algorithm known as PCA (Principal Component Analysis). What is optional for this course is proving that these eigenvectors are the best possible choice—that they maximize captured variance among all projections. sklearn.decomposition.PCA and torch.linalg.eigh return the same rotation-and-scale decomposition.

Eigenfaces: a generative model for images

Everything above works just as well when each “point” is an entire image instead of a pair of coordinates. Flatten a grayscale face photo into a single long vector x—one entry per pixel—and a collection of face photos becomes a cloud of points in a very high-dimensional space, exactly like the scatter plots in Figure 7.2, just with thousands of dimensions instead of two. Fitting a multivariate Gaussian to that cloud, using nothing more than equations (7.6) and (7.12) from above, is the idea behind eigenfaces, introduced by Turk and Pentland (1991).[1]

The mean μ̂ is an actual image—the average face. The principal axes (the columns of R) are also images, once reshaped back to the original height and width; these are the eigenfaces themselves, each one a pattern of pixel-brightness variation shared across the dataset (a furrowed brow, a shift in lighting, a wider jaw). The variances along each axis (the diagonal of S) say how much each pattern actually varies from face to face in the training data—large for the patterns that dominate the dataset, small for subtle or rare ones.

This is exactly the sampling recipe from equation (7.9), applied to images: draw a random z ~ 𝒩(0, I) and compute x = μ̂ + Az. Each entry of z is a random weight on one eigenface; adding the weighted eigenfaces to the mean face produces a brand-new synthetic face—one that never appeared in the training data, but plausibly could have. That makes the multivariate Gaussian fit to face images a genuine generative model: a procedure for producing new, never-seen examples by sampling from a learned distribution, not just for describing or classifying existing ones. In practice only the first few dozen or hundred eigenfaces (the ones with the largest variance) are kept—using Ak, the first k columns of A, and a shorter random z—since the rest mostly capture noise. The generated faces from a Gaussian this simple tend to look blurry and averaged-out; later in the course, more expressive generative models (VAEs, GANs, diffusion models) replace this single Gaussian with something far richer, but the underlying idea—sample a latent z, transform it into data space—is exactly the same one introduced here.
