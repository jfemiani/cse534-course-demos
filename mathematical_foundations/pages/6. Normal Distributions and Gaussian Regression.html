Why continuous distributions?

So far we have used Bernoulli or categorical distributions. These work for discrete outcomes—next-token prediction, classification, or choosing among a fixed set of options. But what if the values you need to model are continuous?

An image is a 2D scalar field of pixel intensities. Object positions are continuous coordinates. A diffusion model gradually transforms a noisy image into a clean one, moving through a continuous space at each step. A voice synthesis model predicts continuous waveform amplitudes. How do you model a distribution over these continuous values?

There are many continuous distributions, but one comes up again and again in generative AI: the normal distribution, also called the Gaussian. It is both simple and powerful. It lets us capture how different continuous variables change together—how pixel intensities at nearby locations are correlated, or how position and velocity of a moving object relate. In later lessons, we will use multivariate Gaussians to understand image generation, latent-space geometry, and how generative models learn correlations in data.

Carl Friedrich Gauss worked with astronomical measurements in the early 1800s. When combining multiple measurements of the same quantity, he wanted to find the value most likely to have produced those observations. He already knew the arithmetic mean worked well in practice, so he asked: what error distribution would make the mean the maximum likelihood estimate? In his 1823 work, Gauss proved that the normal distribution is the only one with this property. This established why the bell-shaped curve appears in measurement error analysis and connected maximum likelihood estimation to the arithmetic mean.

The normal also serves as a local approximation to many more complicated distributions. Near a peak, almost any smooth distribution looks roughly normal—similar to how a Taylor series approximates a function locally with a quadratic. This makes the Gaussian a useful starting point even when the true distribution is more complex.

The normal distribution

The normal distribution describes values that cluster around a typical value while larger deviations become less common. Its mean μ sets the center, and its variance σ2 controls how widely the values spread.

Equation (6.1) uses μ, mu, for the mean. It uses σ, lowercase sigma, for the standard deviation, so σ2 is the variance. The lowercase π is pi, the number approximately 3.14159; unlike the large ∏ in earlier lessons, it does not mean to multiply a sequence of terms. The function exp raises the number e to the expression in parentheses. The equation is a density: higher values of the curve indicate regions where observations are more concentrated.

p(x;μ,σ2)=12πσ2exp(−(x−μ)22σ2)
(6.1)

The standard normal has mean zero and variance one. In Equation (6.2), the symbol ∼ is read “is distributed as,” and the calligraphic 𝒩 names a normal distribution. Start with a standard-normal value Z, multiply it by σ to control the spread, and add μ to move the center. The result X has mean μ and variance σ2.

Z∼𝒩(0,1)X=μ+σZ∼𝒩(μ,σ2)
(6.2)

Properties and applications

  • The Central Limit Theorem helps explain why a sum or average formed from many small independent contributions often has an approximately normal shape, provided no single contribution dominates. It does not say that all data are normal.
  • Maximum entropy: Among continuous distributions with a fixed mean and variance, the normal has the greatest differential entropy. This means it makes the fewest additional assumptions beyond the specified mean and variance.
  • Local approximation: Near a peak, many smooth distributions can be approximated by a normal distribution, similar to how a Taylor series uses a quadratic to approximate a function locally. This makes the Gaussian a useful first-order model even when the true distribution is more complex.
  • Connection to squared error: Taking the logarithm of the normal density turns its exponential expression into a squared-error expression, which is why Gaussian likelihood and least-squares regression produce the same parameter estimates.
  • Correlation structure: The multivariate normal distribution, which we will study in the next lesson, lets us model how continuous variables change together—for example, how nearby pixel values in an image are correlated, or how position and velocity relate in a moving object.

Maximum likelihood estimation

Suppose you observe data points x1, x2, ..., xn drawn independently from a normal distribution. Which values of μ and σ2 make the observed data most probable? Taking the derivative of the log-likelihood and setting it to zero gives the maximum likelihood estimates.

The sample mean is the maximum likelihood estimate for μ:

μ^MLE=1n∑ixi
(6.3)

The sample variance that maximizes likelihood is:

σ^MLE2=1n∑i(xi−μ^MLE)2
(6.4)

However, this estimate is biased—it systematically underestimates the true variance. The unbiased estimator divides by n−1 instead of n. We lose one degree of freedom because we used the data to estimate the mean. Most software uses the unbiased version by default.

Linear regression

Linear regression can be written as a model for the mean of a Gaussian outcome. Suppose we have an input x and an output y. Instead of saying y follows a single fixed line, we model the output as a random variable whose mean depends on x:

Y|x∼𝒩(μ(x),σ2)
(6.5)
μ(x)=mx+b
(6.6)

Here m is the slope, b is the intercept, and σ is the noise scale. The mean depends on m and b, but the variance σ2 does not. This keeps the ordinary regression line but makes the probabilistic model explicit.

Suppose we have data points (x1, y1), (x2, y2), ..., (xn, yn). To find the m and b that maximize the likelihood of the observed y values, we write the likelihood as a product of Gaussian densities and take the logarithm. The exponential in the Gaussian formula contains a squared term. When we take the log, that exponential becomes the expression we need to minimize:

argmaxm,b∏ip(yi|xi)=argminm,b∑i[yi−(mxi+b)μ(xi)]2
(6.7)

The squared term on the right is the residual—the difference between the observed value yi and the predicted value μ(xi). So maximum likelihood estimation for Gaussian-distributed errors gives exactly the same m and b as ordinary least squares.
