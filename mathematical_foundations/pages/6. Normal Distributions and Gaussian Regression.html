The normal distribution

The normal distribution, often called the bell curve, describes values that cluster around a typical value while larger deviations become less common. Its mean sets the center, and its variance controls how widely the values spread.

Normal distributions are useful when many small effects combine—for example, variation in response time across repeated calls to a generative-AI service. They are common models, but not every dataset is normal, so the assumption should be checked rather than treated as an automatic truth.

Equation (6.1) uses μ, mu, for the mean. It uses σ, lowercase sigma, for the standard deviation, so σ2 is the variance. The lowercase π is pi, the number approximately 3.14159; unlike the large ∏ in earlier lessons, it does not mean to multiply a sequence of terms. The function exp raises the number e to the expression in parentheses. The equation is a density: higher values of the curve indicate regions where observations are more concentrated.

The standard normal has mean zero and variance one. In Equation (6.2), the symbol ∼ is read “is distributed as,” and the calligraphic 𝒩 names a normal distribution. Start with a standard-normal value Z, multiply it by σ to control the spread, and add μ to move the center. The result X has mean μ and variance σ2.

Z∼𝒩(0,1)X=μ+σZ∼𝒩(μ,σ2)
(6.2)

Why the normal is useful

  • The Central Limit Theorem helps explain why a total or average formed from many small contributions often has an approximately normal shape, provided no single contribution dominates. It does not say that all data are normal.
  • Lesson 4 introduced entropy for discrete outcomes. Its continuous counterpart is called differential entropy. Among continuous distributions with a fixed mean and variance, the normal has the greatest differential entropy. This fact is useful context but is not required for the quiz.
  • Taking the logarithm of the normal density turns its exponential expression into a squared-error expression, which makes likelihood calculations easier.

Gaussian regression

Linear regression can be written as a generative process. For a GenAI-related example, let X be prompt length and Y be the number of output tokens. The line predicts the typical output length, and Gaussian noise represents the variation among individual responses. In Equation (6.3), β is beta (pronounced “BAY-ta”): β0 is the intercept and β1 is the slope. The symbol ε is epsilon (pronounced “EP-si-lon”) and represents the random error added to the line.

Y=β0+β1X+ε,ε∼𝒩(0,σ2)
(6.3)

A residual is the observed value minus the value predicted by the line. In Equation (6.4), argmax means “the parameter values that make this expression as large as possible,” while argmin means “the parameter values that make this expression as small as possible.” For independent Gaussian residuals with fixed variance, the β0 and β1 that maximize likelihood are exactly the values that minimize the sum of squared residuals. This is why the probability assumption leads to ordinary least squares.

argmaxβ0,β1∏ip(yi|xi)=argminβ0,β1∑i[yi−(β0+β1xi)]2
(6.4)
