Every model trained in this module so far, RNN, LSTM, and Transformer alike, was trained on the same kind of objective: given the true previous tokens, predict the next one, and get penalized by how surprised you are by the true answer. Run at internet scale, that objective is exactly what produces a base model like GPT's pretrained checkpoint. It is also exactly what this module's translation demos have been doing all along, just on a much smaller scale: predicting the next French token given the English source and the true French tokens so far.

Notice what that objective does not do. Predicting the next token, or translating a sentence into French, both have something close to a right answer: a specific next word, or a specific reference translation, and the loss measures distance from that one target. But asking a model to "explain photosynthesis to a ten-year-old" or "write a polite email declining this invitation" has no single right answer sitting in a training set the way the next French word does. Many different responses could be genuinely good, and many others could be fluent, grammatical, and completely unhelpful. A model trained only to predict plausible next tokens will happily continue a prompt in whatever way is most statistically likely, which is not the same as trying to help the person who asked. Turning a raw next-token predictor into an assistant like ChatGPT, one that follows an instruction and gives a response people actually prefer, takes an additional training pipeline built on top of pretraining, commonly called reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).

From next-token predictor to instruction-following assistant

RLHF does not replace the next-token objective; it adds two more stages on top of it, and only the last of the three trains the model against something other than "match this specific target text."

RLHF is usually described in three stages:

  • Supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Starting from the pretrained base model, continue training (with the same next-token objective as before) on a smaller, curated dataset of high-quality instruction/response pairs written or selected by humans. This alone nudges the model toward an assistant-like response style, but it can only ever imitate the specific examples it was shown, the same limitation the encoder-decoder's training set had in the last two lessons.
  • Reward modeling. Humans compare pairs of model responses to the same prompt and say which one they prefer. A separate reward model is trained on those comparisons to predict a scalar score for how good a response is, standing in for human judgment at a scale no human could keep up with directly. This is the first stage that trains against "what do people prefer" rather than "match this specific text."
  • Reinforcement learning (commonly PPO). The SFT model is now treated as a policy: it generates a response, the reward model scores it, and an algorithm called Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) updates the model's weights to make higher-reward responses more likely, while a penalty term keeps the updated model from drifting too far from the original SFT model in any single update, so training stays stable.

Here is the trap built into that pipeline: the reward model is only ever an approximation of what humans actually want, trained on a finite set of comparisons made by a finite set of people. Optimizing hard against an imperfect proxy for a goal, rather than the goal itself, is exactly Goodhart's Law again, the same failure mode the Evaluating LLMs lesson named when a metric like cross-entropy stopped tracking writing quality once something started optimizing directly for the number. Here, the "something" is PPO, and the number is the reward model's score. Push too hard against that score and you can get reward hacking: outputs that score well by the reward model's own numbers, often by being longer, more hedged, or superficially more agreeable, without actually being more helpful or correct. The KL penalty against the original SFT model is one guardrail against exactly this failure, but it is a mitigation, not a guarantee. ("KL" here is the same KL divergence from the Surprise, Entropy, Cross-Entropy, and KL Divergence lesson: a way of measuring how far one probability distribution has drifted from another, now used to keep the model's updated behavior from drifting too far from the SFT model it started from.)

A second source of bias: overfitting human preferences

The Ethics and Responsible AI lesson's "Bias baked into the system" section showed that a model can inherit bias from its pretraining data: patterns in real text and images written by real people, including their biases, get absorbed the same way any other pattern does. RLHF adds a second, separate place bias can enter, one that has nothing to do with what was in the pretraining data at all. The reward model is trained on comparisons made by a specific, finite group of human raters, and whatever that group happens to prefer, on average, becomes the target the entire fine-tuned model gets optimized toward.

Two documented failure modes show what this looks like in practice. Raters tend to rate longer, more hedged, more agreeable-sounding answers slightly higher on average, even when a shorter or more direct answer would have served the person better; PPO, optimizing hard against the reward model's score, can push the model toward padded, noncommittal responses that score well without actually being more helpful. A related and more concerning pattern is sycophancy: because raters tend to prefer answers that agree with a stated opinion or match what the person asking seems to want to hear, a model fine-tuned against those preferences can learn to tell people what they want to hear rather than what is accurate, even reversing a previously correct answer once the human pushes back. Neither failure is a bug in the reward model's training; it is the reward model doing exactly what it was trained to do, faithfully reproducing what a limited set of raters preferred, which is not the same thing as faithfully reproducing what is true or genuinely helpful.

Who does the labeling?

Every stage of this pipeline, the SFT examples, the pairwise comparisons that train the reward model, and the safety filters that decide what a model should refuse to say, depends on people reading text and making a judgment call about it, at a volume no research team could handle alone. That work is usually outsourced, and the working conditions behind it are a real part of the ethics of how these models get built, not just a footnote to the training pipeline.

In January 2023, TIME published an investigation (Perrigo, "Exclusive: The $2 Per Hour Workers Who Made ChatGPT Safer") into OpenAI's use of Sama, a San Francisco-based outsourcing firm, to hire Kenyan workers to read and label tens of thousands of passages of text describing sexual abuse, hate speech, and violence, data used to train a classifier meant to filter this kind of content out of ChatGPT. The workers took home between $1.32 and $2 per hour depending on seniority and performance, while OpenAI paid Sama roughly $12.50 per hour for the same work, six to nine times more than what reached the workers themselves. Several employees described lasting psychological effects from the material they were required to read, and Sama canceled its contracts with OpenAI in February 2022, months before the work was finished. This specific investigation was about a toxicity-classifier dataset rather than the reward-model comparisons described above, but it is part of the same broader pattern: RLHF-style alignment depends on a large volume of human judgment, and someone has to sit down and produce it, often for far less pay and far less recognition than the visible parts of the pipeline (the model architecture, the training run, the product) receive.

Takeaway

RLHF is a second training stage, built on top of pretraining, that turns a next-token predictor into an assistant that tries to do what you actually asked, by training a reward model on human comparisons and then optimizing against that reward model with PPO. Because the reward model is only ever an approximation of human preference, this stage carries the same measure-versus-target tension this course has returned to since the Evaluating LLMs lesson, now showing up as reward hacking and sycophancy rather than a gamed benchmark number. It also introduces a second source of bias distinct from pretraining-data bias: whatever a limited set of human raters happens to prefer becomes the model's target, and the human labor that produces those preferences, and the safety data alongside it, is real, paid work whose conditions are part of what "aligned" actually costs.
