The LSTM fixed part of the RNN's problem: gating let the network protect information across more steps than a plain RNN could. But even with gating, the decoder still starts from exactly one vector, whatever the encoder's last step happened to produce, and that vector still has to stand in for the entire source sentence. What if the decoder did not have to rely on a single summary at all, and could instead look directly at any word of the source sentence, every single time it generates an output word?

That is attention, and it is the core idea behind the Transformer, the architecture behind GPT, ChatGPT, and every other modern large language model. This lesson trains a small Transformer on the exact same task, data, and evaluation sentences as the last two lessons, then looks directly at its attention weights to see what "looking at any word" actually means in practice.

Attention: a learned, per-step lookup

Recall the Chunking and Retrieval lesson's demos: given a query, you compared it against a set of candidate vectors using a dot-product similarity score, then used those scores to decide which candidates mattered. Attention is that same idea, but learned end-to-end and used inside the network itself, at every decoding step. At each step, the decoder builds a query from what it has generated so far and compares it against a key vector for every position in the source sentence, producing one similarity score per position.

Those scores are turned into a set of weights that sum to 1 using a softmax, and the name is worth pausing on. A hard "max" would simply pick out the single most relevant key and discard every other score entirely, but a hard, all-or-nothing pick like that has nothing for gradient descent to learn from: nudging a losing score up or down would never change which key gets picked, right up until the instant it suddenly overtook the winner, so there is no gradient connecting a small change in the scores to a small change in the outcome anywhere in between. Softmax is a soft version of that same selection: every key keeps some share of the weight, however small, so the network can learn, through the same backpropagation this course has used all along, exactly how much each key should matter, rather than being forced into an unlearnable hard choice.

Those softmax weights are then used to combine the corresponding value vectors into a single, freshly-computed summary, one that can be completely different at every decoding step, unlike the encoder-decoder's one fixed vector.

Attention did not start with the Transformer. Bahdanau et al. first added it to an RNN encoder-decoder in 2015, years before Transformers existed, as a fix for exactly the same fixed-vector bottleneck the last two lessons ran into: instead of handing the decoder only the encoder's final hidden state, mix together all of the encoder's hidden states, weighted by how relevant each one is to the word being generated right now.

The version used in Transformers replaces that mixing scheme with the dot-product query/key/value mechanism described above: every encoder position gets its own learned key and value vector, the decoder's current position gets a learned query vector, and the weights come from a scaled dot product between query and key instead of a small learned neural network.

One way to keep query, key, and value straight: if you wanted to know whether to pet an unfamiliar cat, you might ask a horse expert, a house-cat expert, and a bird expert. Your question (“should I pet this cat?”) is the query; each expert's area of expertise is their key, what determines how relevant their opinion is to this particular question; and each expert's actual answer is their value, what actually gets used once you have decided how much to weight it.

Query, key, value attention is the mechanism at the center of a 2017 paper from Google, Attention Is All You Need, which introduced the Transformer. The title is a direct claim: every prior sequence model, RNN and LSTM included, relied on recurrence, passing a hidden state forward one step at a time, to let information move across a sequence. This paper's argument was that attention alone, with no recurrence anywhere in the network, was enough to do the job, and the diagram below is the architecture that paper proposed.

The full diagram has a lot of boxes in it, and this lesson does not need you to trace every one of them. What matters is the two halves. The paper was built and evaluated on the same kind of task this lesson's demo uses, translation, and the two halves map directly onto the encoder-decoder split from the last two lessons. The left half is the encoder. Because the encoder's job is only to read the source sentence, the entire source sentence is available to it at once, both during training and at translation time; there is nothing yet to predict, so there is nothing that needs to stay hidden. Reading it all at once through the attention mechanism above turns the source sentence into a rich set of key and value vectors that the decoder can look back at.

The right half is the decoder, and it is autoregressive, exactly like the RNN and LSTM decoders in the last two lessons: it generates the output sentence one word at a time, and each new word is conditioned only on the words already generated before it. During training, teacher forcing means the entire true output sentence is already known in advance, so it might seem like the decoder could simply look at all of it at once, the same way the encoder does with the source sentence. But at translation time, there is no true output sentence to look at; the decoder only has whatever it has generated so far, and it genuinely cannot see the words it has not produced yet. Training and translation would give the decoder different information unless something forces training to respect that same restriction. Removing recurrence is what makes training fast: with no hidden state to carry forward one step at a time, an entire sentence's worth of teacher-forced predictions can be computed in one parallel pass instead of one word at a time. But that same parallel pass, if left unrestricted, would let an early word's prediction attend to later words it is not supposed to have seen yet, which never happens at translation time, since those later words do not exist yet. The fix is a masked version of attention, applied only inside the decoder, that blocks every position from attending to any position after it, so training sees exactly the same restricted view of the sentence that translation does. That masking mechanism is covered in detail further down this page, once the demo's own decoder code makes it concrete.

The code below is available in full on GitHub at 05_transformer_mt.py. The encoder here is just PyTorch's built-in nn.TransformerEncoder; nothing hand-rolled. The decoder is built from PyTorch's built-in nn.MultiheadAttention blocks directly, rather than PyTorch's built-in nn.TransformerDecoderLayer, for one specific reason: doing it this way makes the cross-attention weights easy to pull out afterward for the visualization later in this lesson. The attention math itself is entirely PyTorch's; nothing about the computation is hand-rolled.

Notice there is no hidden state being carried from step to step anywhere in that method. Every call recomputes cross-attention fresh, over every encoder output position at once. That is the whole architectural difference from the last two lessons: no recurrence, no fixed-size bottleneck vector, just a learned lookup, recomputed at every step.

That single-step view is how the Transformer decoder runs at translation time, one word at a time, exactly like the RNN and LSTM decoders before it. Training is different, and it is the other half of why Transformers are fast to train: because teacher forcing already supplies every true previous word, the decoder can process an entire target sentence in one parallel pass instead of stepping through it word by word. train_epoch() above does exactly this, in the line decoder(tgt[:, :-1], ...), feeding the whole shifted sentence in at once.

The only thing keeping that parallel pass honest is causal masking: the self-attention step's causal_mask blocks every position from attending to positions after it, so the prediction for word 3 still only ever sees words 1 and 2, the same information it would have had if it were generated one step at a time.

Look at the three steps inside that method in order. Self-attention comes first: the decoder attends over its own previous output words, the same query/key/value mechanism described above, but applied to what the decoder has generated so far rather than to the source sentence, so each position can take into account the words that came before it. Cross-attention comes second: this is the step that looks at the source sentence, using the decoder's state as the query and the encoder's output as both keys and values, which is what actually removes the fixed-size bottleneck. A feedforward layer comes last, a plain two-layer neural network (visible as self.ff in the code above) applied independently at every position, giving the model some extra capacity to transform each position's representation after attention has gathered the relevant information.

Two more pieces make the Transformer work that the code walkthrough above does not show. First, attention has no built-in sense of order: the dot product between a query and a key does not change if you shuffle the positions around, unlike an RNN, which reads its input strictly left to right. To fix that, a positional encoding vector, one per position in the sequence, is added directly to each position's input embedding before anything else happens, so the network can tell the first word from the seventh. Second, rather than computing attention once per layer, the Transformer computes it several times in parallel, each with its own learned query, key, and value weight matrices; this is multi-head attention, and each of these parallel “heads” is free to learn a different notion of relevance (one head might track subject-verb agreement, another might track nearby adjectives) before all of the heads' outputs are concatenated and passed through one more learned projection.

But look at the per-sentence reference losses across all three lessons' tables: the Transformer only has the single lowest reference loss on one sentence, “I know my job.” (1.686). On the other four sentences, including the long eight-word one, the LSTM's reference loss is the lowest of the three, and on the long sentence specifically the Transformer scores 9.036, worse than both the RNN (6.881) and the LSTM (6.538). This is the same lesson the Evaluating LLMs lesson taught with the "cherries, apples, and lemons" demo: one averaged number, even a clearly-improving one, can hide individual examples that go the other way. The Transformer's win here is real, but it is a win in aggregate validation loss, not a clean sweep of every sentence; a small model trained on a few thousand noisy sentence pairs will have off days on individual sentences no matter which architecture you pick, so judge the architecture by the pattern across many examples, not by any single one.

Clean word-for-word alignment is a property you would expect to see more reliably from a much larger model trained on far more data, something worth keeping in mind the next time you see a beautifully clean attention-heatmap figure in a paper.

Takeaway

Attention replaces a single fixed-size summary vector with a fresh, learned lookup at every decoding step, computed directly from the source sentence rather than from a bottleneck. On this module's small-scale task, that produced the best aggregate validation loss of the three architectures, while still showing, on one sentence, that a single example can defy the aggregate trend, and that attention alignment at this scale is a real mechanism, not yet a clean picture. But every model trained in this module so far, Transformer included, was trained the same way: predict the next token and be penalized by how surprised you are by the true answer. The next page, 6.7, looks at the additional training stage that turns a next-token predictor like this one into an assistant that actually tries to do what you asked.
