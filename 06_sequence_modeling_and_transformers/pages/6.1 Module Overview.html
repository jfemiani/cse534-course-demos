The N-Gram Language Generation lesson built a language model from character counts. The Tool Use and Retrieval and Evaluation and Ethics modules showed you how to steer, retrieve for, and evaluate a model you didn't train yourself. This module asks a different question: how do you actually build a model that reads one sequence and produces another? That is the problem behind translation, summarization, and, eventually, every chat model you have used.

Before any of that, though, two questions have to be answered first: how does a model turn raw text into discrete units it can process at all (tokenization), and how does it turn those units into vectors that capture what they mean (word embeddings)? Every architecture later in this module depends on both. From there, every remaining lesson trains the same task on the same data: translating short English sentences into French. What changes from lesson to lesson is the architecture. You will train a recurrent network (RNN), then a gated recurrent network (LSTM), then a Transformer (the architecture behind GPT, ChatGPT, and every other modern large language model) on the identical corpus and the identical five evaluation sentences.

By the end of this module, you will be able to

  • Explain why a fixed, word-level vocabulary always has an out-of-vocabulary problem, and why subword tokenization (BPE) avoids it entirely by building a vocabulary of pieces from the bottom up.
  • Contrast a hand-built symbolic lookup for word meaning (WordNet) with a learned distributional vector (word2vec, GloVe), and use PCA to actually look at a handful of high-dimensional word embeddings.
  • Explain why a fixed-context model (the n-gram model from the N-Gram Language Generation lesson) cannot represent arbitrary-length dependencies, and why a model needs a state that persists across the whole sequence to do so.
  • Train a recurrent encoder-decoder (RNN) on a real sequence-to-sequence task, and diagnose the specific way it fails as sentences get longer.
  • Train a gated (LSTM) encoder-decoder on the identical task and data, and explain mechanistically why gating extends a model's effective memory.
  • Train a small Transformer encoder-decoder on the identical task, and explain attention as a learned, per-step weighted lookup over the whole source sentence, not a compressed summary vector.
  • Describe the pipeline (supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, and reinforcement learning via PPO, short for Proximal Policy Optimization) that turns a pretrained next-token predictor into an instruction-following assistant like ChatGPT.
  • Compare RNN, LSTM, and Transformer translation quality on the same held-out sentences, and state which architectural property explains each gap.

One corpus, three architectures

Every demo in this module is built on the same real, if noisy, data: a few thousand English-French sentence pairs streamed from the Hugging Face Helsinki-NLP/opus-100 dataset. All three lessons use PyTorch (this module's framework, not the Keras/TensorFlow code in the GDL book) and the same five held-out sentences for evaluation, including one deliberately long one. Watch that long sentence across the module: an RNN squeezes an entire sentence into one fixed-size vector and starts to lose the thread. An LSTM's gates help, but the bottleneck is still there. A Transformer removes the bottleneck entirely; at every step, the decoder can look directly at any word in the source sentence.
