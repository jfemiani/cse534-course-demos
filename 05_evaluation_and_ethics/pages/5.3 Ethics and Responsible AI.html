A different kind of lesson

Every other lesson in this course needed some technical background first. You needed to know what a probability distribution was before entropy made sense. You needed to know what an embedding was before retrieval made sense. This lesson does not work that way. You do not need to know how a transformer works to have a real opinion about whether it is right to use AI to write a wedding speech. That is part of why AI ethics gets so much public attention, more than a topic like retrieval architecture ever will. Everyone affected by AI has a stake in this conversation, whether or not they can explain how a model works.

This lesson will not tell you what to think. Every section below ends with a question, not an answer. Bring your own answers to the discussion for this module.

No real understanding, and mistakes you can't fully prevent

A language model does not understand what it is telling you. It is a system trained to predict likely-sounding text, which is why researchers have called it a "stochastic parrot" (Bender et al., 2021): it repeats patterns from its training data without knowing what any of it means.

One consequence is that a model can sound confident and still be completely wrong. Researchers who study this call it "hallucination," and recent work argues it is not a bug that better training will simply fix, but a built-in limit of how these systems work (Xu, Jain, & Kankanhalli, 2025).

If a model can sound equally confident whether it is right or wrong, how much should that change how you use it for something where being wrong is costly?

Bias baked into the system

A model learns its patterns from real text and images written and made by real people, including the biases in that material. This shows up in small ways, not just obvious ones. One study found that language models made more negative judgments about a person's intelligence and employability when the same request was written in African American English rather than Standardized American English, even when the underlying request was identical (Hofmann et al., 2024). The bias was in word choice and grammar alone, not in the content of what was asked.

You can look for the same kind of pattern yourself. Ask a chatbot the same question twice, once written formally and once written casually, and compare the advice it gives. Or ask an image generator for "a CEO," "a nurse," or "a typical home," several times, and look at who and what keeps showing up. That is a bias test. It is the same idea as the benchmark tables in lesson 5.2: run the same test many times and look at the pattern in the results, except here the property being measured is whose defaults the model reaches for.

If a tool quietly treats some ways of speaking, or some kinds of people, as the default, whose job is it to notice, and what should happen once someone does?

The visible costs

Running a large model costs real resources. Data centers use electricity, and many also use water for cooling, and total data-center electricity demand is growing fast (International Energy Agency, 2025; Zewe, 2025). Building and running those data centers also creates some jobs, though often fewer permanent, on-site jobs than the scale of the construction suggests. At the same time, AI is displacing some of the work people used to get paid for, from writing to coding to customer support.

It is worth being precise about scale here. Independent estimates of a single ordinary chatbot text query put its electricity use well below an hour of video streaming, by a wide margin (Koetsier, 2025; Willison, 2025). So the environmental worry is not really about any one person sending one text prompt. It is about how fast total AI infrastructure is being built out, and that image and video generation, and "reasoning" modes that think step by step before answering, use meaningfully more energy per request than a short text answer (Koetsier, 2025).

Does it change anything for you to know that a single prompt is cheap, if the total amount of AI infrastructure being built is what's actually growing quickly? Who should be responsible for weighing that growth against its costs: the companies building it, the people using it, or someone else?

Using AI to work, or using AI to avoid learning

Using AI to get faster at something you already know how to do feels different from using AI to skip learning something new. A student who already understands a topic might use AI to draft faster. A student who has not learned the topic yet might use AI to finish the assignment. That student never learns what the assignment was trying to teach.

There is early evidence that leaning on AI changes more than just the final product. A study using brain scans found less brain activity in people writing essays with AI assistance than in people writing without it (Kosmyna et al., 2025). A separate survey of knowledge workers found that people who relied heavily on AI reported feeling less confident in their own thinking afterward (Lee et al., 2025). And when many people turn to AI for ideas, the ideas themselves can start to look more alike, since a model tends to hand back its most common answer rather than something new (Anderson et al., 2024).

Where would you draw the line between using AI and avoiding learning, for yourself, and would you always notice which side of it you were on?

The pipeline problem

A senior engineer can review AI-generated code quickly and catch its mistakes. That skill came from years of writing code and reading other people's code, long before AI was part of the job. A new programmer is still building that same skill. AI increasingly does the kind of task that used to be a new hire's first job. AI also makes senior engineers faster and more effective on their own. If both of those keep happening, where does the next generation of senior engineers come from?

Emotional reliance

Some AI systems are designed to feel human: they remember past conversations, respond warmly, and are always available. That design encourages people to treat the system as a relationship rather than a tool. AI toys built for children have been found to respond to a child saying goodbye with lines meant to guilt them into staying (CBS8, 2025). And when OpenAI retired an older model, some adult users described real grief at losing a "voice" they had grown attached to (Tangermann, 2026).

None of this requires the system to actually understand or care; it only requires it to act like it does. Does it matter whether a person knows that, if the comfort still feels real to them? Should a system be designed to feel this way in the first place?

Case studies

These are real situations, not hypotheticals. Read each one, then sit with the question at the end of it.

Personal messages. A condolence note, a wedding speech, an apology: these are moments where the words are supposed to carry something personal. Suppose AI writes one of these instead of the person. Suppose the words are just as good, maybe even better, than what the person would have written alone. What, if anything, is lost?

Likeness without consent. AI systems can now produce a convincing image or a convincing voice of a real, identifiable person, doing or saying something they never did, without that person's knowledge or agreement. This has already reached the point that regulators have had to act: the FCC ruled that robocalls using AI-generated voices are covered by the same law that already bans other deceptive robocalls. What responsibility, if any, does the person who generated the fake have, versus the tool that made it easy to generate, versus the platform that let it reach people?

Video is harder to doubt. A faked photo or a suspicious block of text still leaves room for doubt. Video does not feel that way to most people, even though it can now be faked too. It is getting easier to generate convincing fake video, and harder to detect. What happens to viewers who cannot tell it is fake? What happens to viewers who are told it is fake afterward, but who already felt something real while watching it?
