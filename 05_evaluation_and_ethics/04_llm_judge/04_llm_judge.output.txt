first-turn answer:
Economic indicators such as GDP growth, inflation, and unemployment are interconnected, but their relationships vary with the business cycle, supply conditions, expectations, and policy credibility.

## Core relationships

### GDP and unemployment
GDP growth and unemployment usually move in opposite directions:

- When real GDP grows faster than the economy’s productive capacity, firms generally hire more workers, reducing unemployment.
- During recessions, falling demand and output lead firms to cut hours and jobs, raising unemployment.
- This relationship is often described by **Okun’s law**: unusually weak output growth tends to be associated with rising unemployment.

The relationship is not exact. Productivity changes, labor-force participation, migration, and firms’ decisions to adjust hours rather than headcount can weaken it.

### Inflation and unemployment
In the short run, inflation and unemployment can exhibit an inverse relationship known as the **Phillips curve**:

- Strong demand can lower unemployment while putting upward pressure on wages and prices.
- Weak demand can raise unemployment and reduce inflationary pressure.

However, this trade-off is neither stable nor permanent. If people expect continued inflation, workers and firms adjust wages and prices accordingly. In the long run, attempts to hold unemployment below its sustainable or “natural” rate may mainly produce higher inflation rather than lasting employment gains.

Supply shocks can also cause **stagflation**—high inflation alongside weak GDP growth and high unemployment. Examples include sharp increases in energy costs or major supply-chain disruptions.

### GDP and inflation
Their correlation depends on the source of the economic change:

- **Demand-driven expansion:** GDP and inflation often rise together.
- **Demand-driven recession:** GDP growth and inflation tend to decline together.
- **Positive supply shock:** GDP may rise while inflation falls.
- **Negative supply shock:** GDP may fall while inflation rises.

Therefore, inflation cannot be interpreted solely as evidence that the economy is growing too quickly; it may also reflect production constraints or imported costs.

## Effects of fiscal policy

Fiscal policy involves government spending, taxation, and transfers.

### Expansionary fiscal policy
Examples include increased public spending, tax cuts, and larger transfers.

Typical effects:

- Raises aggregate demand and real GDP, especially when the economy has unused capacity.
- Reduces cyclical unemployment as firms respond to stronger demand.
- Can increase inflation if demand grows faster than productive capacity.
- May raise interest rates or public debt, potentially crowding out some private investment.

The impact is usually larger when unemployment is high, interest rates are low, households spend rather than save additional income, and monetary policy does not offset the stimulus.

### Contractionary fiscal policy
Examples include spending cuts or tax increases.

Typical effects:

- Reduces aggregate demand and inflationary pressure.
- Slows GDP growth in the short run.
- Can raise unemployment.
- May improve public finances and reduce pressure on interest rates over time.

Fiscal measures can also affect the supply side. Infrastructure, education, and well-designed investment incentives may expand productive capacity, allowing higher GDP with less inflation. Conversely, poorly targeted spending or persistent deficits in an already overheated economy can intensify inflation.

**Automatic stabilizers**, such as progressive taxes and unemployment benefits, also moderate cycles without new legislation: tax receipts fall and benefits rise during recessions, supporting demand, with the reverse occurring during expansions.

## Effects of monetary policy

Monetary policy is conducted mainly through policy interest rates, liquidity operations, asset purchases, and communication about future policy.

### Expansionary monetary policy
A central bank may cut interest rates or increase financial-system liquidity.

Typical transmission:

1. Borrowing becomes cheaper.
2. Consumption, housing demand, and business investment increase.
3. Asset prices and credit availability may rise.
4. The currency may depreciate, supporting exports but making imports more expensive.
5. GDP growth strengthens and unemployment tends to decline.
6. Inflation usually increases after a lag.

Expansionary policy is most effective when borrowers are willing and able to take on credit. It may be less effective during severe financial stress or when rates are already near their lower bound.

### Contractionary monetary policy
A central bank may raise interest rates or reduce liquidity.

Typical effects:

- Weakens interest-sensitive spending and investment.
- Slows credit creation and aggregate demand.
- Moderates GDP growth and may increase unemployment.
- Reduces inflation over time.
- May strengthen the currency, lowering import prices but weakening exports.

Monetary policy operates with uncertain and sometimes long lags. Tightening may reduce inflation only after economic activity has already slowed.

## Simplified policy comparison

| Policy action | Short-run GDP | Unemployment | Inflation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Higher government spending or tax cuts | Usually rises | Usually falls | Usually rises |
| Spending cuts or tax increases | Usually falls | Usually rises | Usually falls |
| Lower interest rates | Usually rises | Usually falls | Usually rises |
| Higher interest rates | Usually falls | Usually rises | Usually falls |

These are typical demand-side effects, not guarantees.

## Key qualifications

- **Demand shocks and supply shocks produce different correlations.**
- **Nominal GDP** can rise simply because prices rise; **real GDP** is the better measure of output growth.
- Low unemployment is not necessarily inflationary if productivity or labor supply is expanding.
- Policy affects the economy with lags, so acting on current data can lead to over- or under-tightening.
- Fiscal and monetary policies interact. Fiscal stimulus may be partly offset if the central bank raises rates, while coordinated support can have a larger effect during a deep recession.
- Long-run prosperity depends more on productivity, labor-force growth, institutions, investment, and technology than on repeated demand stimulus.

In summary, stronger demand generally raises GDP, lowers unemployment, and increases inflationary pressure. Fiscal and monetary policies influence these outcomes by changing total spending, borrowing conditions, expectations, and productive capacity, but the final effect depends heavily on whether the economy is demand-constrained, supply-constrained, or experiencing both.

eli5 answer:
Imagine the economy is a big lemonade stand:

- **GDP** is how much lemonade the stand makes and sells.
- **Unemployment** is how many people want to help but do not have a job.
- **Inflation** means the price of lemonade and other things is going up.

### How they connect

When lots of people buy lemonade:

- The stand makes more lemonade, so **GDP rises**.
- It needs more helpers, so **unemployment falls**.
- If demand is greater than the stand can handle, it may raise prices, so **inflation rises**.

When fewer people buy lemonade:

- The stand makes less, so **GDP falls**.
- It needs fewer helpers, so **unemployment rises**.
- Prices usually rise more slowly, so **inflation falls**.

But sometimes ingredients become scarce or expensive. Then the stand may make less lemonade while charging more. That means **GDP can fall and inflation can rise at the same time**.

### What the government can do

**Fiscal policy** means changing government spending and taxes.

- To help a slow economy, the government can spend more or lower taxes. People then have more money to spend, businesses sell more, and they may hire workers. But too much spending can push prices up.
- To cool an economy with rapidly rising prices, the government can spend less or raise taxes. This may reduce inflation, but it can also slow production and hiring.

### What the central bank can do

**Monetary policy** mostly means changing interest rates—the extra cost of borrowing money.

- **Lower interest rates** make it cheaper to borrow. Families and businesses tend to spend more, helping GDP and jobs grow. Prices may also rise faster.
- **Higher interest rates** make borrowing more expensive. People tend to spend less, which can slow inflation, but it may also reduce GDP growth and increase unemployment.

So, in simple terms: policymakers can press the economy’s **gas pedal** to encourage spending and jobs, or the **brake pedal** to control rising prices. The hard part is choosing the right pressure without making the economy too hot or too cold.
